# BreakBoss pattern research

Research date: 11 October 2026. Purpose: turn the requested genres and producer references into original, repeatable drum phrases using the uploaded Multiverse sounds.

## What was studied

Public music-production articles, producer interviews, indexed YouTube tutorial descriptions and readable producer-video transcripts were studied. Direct YouTube playback requests were throttled or unavailable. No claim is made that those videos or commercial recordings were heard, decoded, or transcribed by ear. ChordU mirrors provide text associated with the original Genius videos; their automatic chord labels and tempo estimates were **not** used as musical evidence. This research does not establish an exact signature beat for any producer.

The supplied public conversation was recovered and searched. It contains Drum Loop Mode requirements but no specific song, YouTube, or reference-loop links. The uploaded 90 BPM roll waveforms were measured separately; see LOOP_STUDY.md. Any additional reference from a different conversation was not available here.

## Implementation conclusions

Use musical motifs rather than independent random hits in each bar. Repeat a recognizable main idea, change an answer near the end of a phrase, and keep long stretches of negative space. Create 2-, 4- and 8-bar phrases with a controlled final-bar fill or omission. Timing offsets, note lengths, velocities, pitch choices, and roll events should all be computed before playback; no generative work or expensive new effects belong in the render callback.

Genre settings below are **original design choices informed by the sources**, not published rules or transcriptions. A tempo remains user-adjustable. A family can sound convincing across a range, and city labels do not define a single universal drum grid.

| Family | Suggested starting tempo | Original phrase design |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atlanta / modern trap | 140–154 BPM | Half-time backbeat on beat 3; sparse syncopated kicks; an eighth-note hat motif with short triplet or 32nd-note answers, velocity slopes and deliberate hat rests. Short percussion replies occupy the gaps. Bass does not blindly double every kick. |
| Memphis-influenced trap | 136–150 BPM | Half-time foundation with tighter repeated kick/bass groups, clipped snare texture, a recurring dry cowbell/rim motif and occasional triplet grouping. Keep it distinct from full-tempo drift phonk; a cowbell is optional rather than a historical requirement. |
| Toronto / atmospheric trap | 124–142 BPM, or 62–71 felt | Fewer kicks, longer spaces after the backbeat, quieter sparse hats, muted rim/snaps and a short late-phrase response. Bass anchors the first bar, answers the second and leaves vocal space. |
| Neo soul | 76–94 BPM | Backbeat on 2 and 4; kick anchors plus understated syncopated answers; a recurring swung hat cell; low snare ghosts; subtly late backbeat. Different lanes use different timing rather than global random jitter. |
| Boom bap | 86–96 BPM | Strong 2-and-4 snare; a short recurring kick motif; restrained swung hats; low ghost/pickup kicks; occasional open hat near a phrase boundary. Preserve the kick/snare focus. |
| Lo-fi | 70–86 BPM | A reduced boom-bap/neo-soul phrase with fewer ornaments, lower hat velocity, restrained lane offsets and shorter, darker samples. Texture is prepared offline rather than a permanent expensive playback effect. |
| Contemporary R&B / trap soul | 72–96 BPM, or doubled | Snap/rim-led restrained backbeat, a quiet hat pattern with pauses, syncopated kicks, and bass phrases with varied lengths. Add an extra snare layer or reply only in the second half of a phrase. |
| 80s electronic soul / pop | 94–116 BPM | A steady drum-machine identity with mild swing, recurring offbeat percussion and a memorable two-bar kick pattern. Regular pulse and controlled fills remain more prominent than trap rolls. |
| 90s pop / new jack swing | 100–116 BPM | More assertive 2-and-4 backbeat, visibly shuffled 16th-note hats, kick/percussion syncopation, short bass notes and repeatable phrase-end answers. |
| Amapiano-inspired | 110–115 BPM | Soft recurring kick pulse with selective omissions; alternating shaker accents; offbeat timekeeping; short, tuned bass/log-drum-inspired call-and-response and syncopated percussion. The supplied 808/percussion can be processed into a related timbre, but should be described as inspired rather than an authentic sampled log drum. |
| Experimental / world percussion | 90–118 BPM | Complementary conga/tom/rim/shaker motifs, rests between answers, occasional cross-rhythm and sparse bass anchors. Retain a clear timekeeper and bound overlapping note density. |

CPU and continuity recommendations: prepare sample tone/envelope/drive/pitch variants once; use the existing sampler for all factory playback; bound simultaneous voices and roll density; avoid long risers or fixed-speed 90 BPM hat-roll recordings as ordinary hat one-shots. Event-level rolls follow the selected tempo and require no real-time time-stretching. At 128 frames/48 kHz the hardware deadline is about 2.67 ms; architectural restraint supports that target, but actual iPad performance must be measured.

## Evidence and useful references

### Genre technique

1. [How to Make Neo Soul Drums — UJAM, Harry Lodes, 26 January 2021](https://www.ujam.com/tutorials/how-to-make-neo-soul-drums/). **Full article text read.** Supports 2-and-4 snare/cross-stick backbeat, widely variable kick placement, small timing/velocity edits, swing and multi-measure variation. It warns that excessive density undermines the relaxed feel. Dry drums, controlled tails and restrained saturation are useful sound-design observations. Application: repeatable pocket with ghost notes and lane-specific timing; do not make every drum late.

2. [90s Boom Bap Hip Hop — Attack Magazine, 8 May 2021](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/90s-boom-bap-hip-hop/). **Full article text read; embedded audio not heard.** Highlights kick/snare focus, velocity sensitivity, a clap nudged slightly late and different groove settings for hats versus kick/snare. It uses MPC 3000 eighth-note 57 and sixteenth-note 74 groove presets rather than one universal global swing number. Application: independent lane timing and understated pick-ups, not wholesale copying of its beat grid or melodic sample.

3. [Drunk Drummer-Style Grooves — Attack Magazine, Aykan Esen, 9 September 2020](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/drunk-drummer-style-grooves/). **Full article text read.** Its example is 90 BPM with custom swing; it explains combining quintuplet, sextuplet and septuplet placements, including 3:2 or 4:3 hat ratios. Backbeats remain on 2 and 4 while other notes fill available space. Application: controlled recurring off-grid relationships; no need for arbitrary random timing or a copied tutorial sequence.

4. [What Is Amapiano Music? An In-depth Guide With Kooldrink — Splice, Harrison Shimazu, 1 March 2025](https://splice.com/blog/what-is-amapiano-music/). **Full article and interview text read; linked YouTube tutorial located.** Kooldrink emphasizes a pitched log drum, moving percussion groove and an audible timekeeper. The article gives 110–115 BPM as a common range. Its bass advice links sub and log-drum envelopes and phase. Application: short tuned bass phrases, offbeat timekeeping and complementary percussion; prepare tone and envelopes offline. [Linked tutorial: How To Make Amapiano w/ Kooldrink](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eRuv90hkBow) was located, with direct video access throttled.

5. [’80s Electronic Soul — Attack Magazine](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/80s-electronic-soul/). **Full introductory/technique text read.** The tutorial takes electronic soul as its reference, with 94 BPM and mild 52% sixteenth swing. It describes a looser kick pattern and percussion/snare that can provide rhythmic motion beyond the normal backbeat role. Application: sparse electronic soul and regular timekeeping rather than assuming all 80s pop is four-on-the-floor.

6. [Teddy Riley and New Jack Swing: An Oral History — Red Bull Music Academy, Chris Daly and Zilla Rocca, 4 May 2017](https://daily.redbullmusicacademy.com/2017/05/teddy-riley-new-jack-swing-oral-history/). **Interview text read.** Participants emphasize the drums' prominence, strong snare character, sparse arrangement, swing exploration and Riley's hands-on performance. Application: more assertive, danceable shuffled backbeat and short syncopated bass/percussion answers. The proposed tempo and event positions are our design choices.

7. [Trap Drum Patterns — Studio Brootle](https://www.studiobrootle.com/trap-drum-patterns/). **Page text reviewed.** It discusses a faster numerical tempo with a sparse half-time feel and contrasts it with hats/fills. Application: a beat-3 backbeat with concise rolls and gaps; keep speed and perceived pulse separate.

## Practical acceptance criteria for the generator

- Phrase identity survives variations: anchor notes and a recurring hat/percussion cell remain recognizable.
- Trap rolls are short ornaments, with capped event counts and no fixed-speed audio phrase substituted for a hat hit.
- Neo-soul/boom-bap timing differences recur in the same musical places across a phrase; they are not fresh random noise every bar.
- R&B uses bass duration and silence as part of the groove; the next kick need not retrigger a bass note.
- Amapiano-inspired bass uses short note lengths and syncopated pitch replies; the pulse remains audible.
- Phrase-end fills answer the main groove and preserve space before the next downbeat.
- Factory event generation happens off the render thread and remains deterministic for a given seed.
- Reference links document inspiration; factory loops are original generated patterns, not exact copies of commercial recordings.
